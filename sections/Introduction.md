# Introduction

The field of artificial intelligence is rife with theories and conceptualization about what intelligence "is" \citep{chollet2019,morris2023,waterhouse2023}. With neural networks specifically, a longstanding issue is how models "overfit" their training set, and thus their performance across various tasks ("intelligence") lacks the powerful generalizability that cognitive entities like humans have. In pursuit of this generalizability, there is an implicit and subtle assumption that in neural networks, much like humans, cognitive abilities are structured according to a causal hierarchy. A popular notion of general intelligence in the field is the concept "fluid intelligence" borrowed from psychometric research, defined loosely as the ability to perform well in any tasks regardless of content (i.e., a "domain-free" cognitive capacity). By definition this construct holds a causal relation, where an increase of fluid intelligence leads to an increase in the mastery of every other cognitive task, so domain-general ability *precedes and builds* domain-specific abilities. Yet this assumption is rarely tested directly, and where it has been, only at a much smaller scale.

Prior work has tried applying factor analysis to benchmark scores, but always at a much smaller scale, and often by fitting a human-informed structure rather than discovering one bottom-up. \citet{ilicagignac2024} fit confirmatory factor analysis to a human-informed causal structure, \citet{burnell2023} found three major factors via EFA without testing for a higher-order $G$, and \citet{krakauer2026,federiakin2025,haznitrama2026} each report a unified general factor at similarly small scale. A common weakness across these studies is the relatively few number of benchmarks or variables, ranging from 10 to 23, most of which measure "smartness" narrowly (mathematics, academic knowledge, common-sense reasoning) and which risk conflating the extracted capability factor with model scale \citep{kearns2026}.

Within the context of prior work, our study presents an unprecedentedly large factor analysis of benchmark scores. The collection comprises 1,618 models and 456 benchmarks over 13,251 evaluation scores. Crucially, our pool of benchmarks covers a highly diverse set of tasks, including those quite outside of the mainstream. Covering only a few popular benchmarks like prior work does is problematic. First, given their popularity, these benchmarks may well be correlated due to what we call a common-investment bias, since organizations expend more effort to perform well specifically on them. Second, there should be no discrimination as to what tasks are important and which aren't, since a task appearing miscellaneous is no reason to exclude it. A useful analogy is that reaction time in humans is correlated to intelligence \citep{kranzler1989}, showing that no matter how seemingly unimportant a task may be, it may provide information entirely unintuitive to our subjective understanding. Across this analysis, we find that factor patterns are only partially interpretable and often incoherent, and that no silver-bullet "general intelligence" factor exists, meaning a generally-intelligent language model cannot be reached by targeting a single conceptual ability alone.
